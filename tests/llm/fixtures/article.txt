Diffusion Models: A Practical Introduction

Diffusion models are a class of generative models that learn to produce data by
reversing a gradual noising process. During training, Gaussian noise is added to
a data point over many steps until it becomes pure noise. A neural network is
then trained to predict and remove that noise one step at a time. At generation
time, the model starts from random noise and iteratively denoises it into a
coherent sample.

The forward process is fixed and requires no learning: at each timestep t, a
small amount of noise is added according to a variance schedule. The reverse
process is where learning happens. The network is typically a U-Net that takes a
noisy sample and the timestep as input and predicts the noise component. The
training objective reduces to a simple mean-squared error between the predicted
and actual noise.

Compared to GANs, diffusion models are more stable to train and cover the data
distribution more faithfully, at the cost of slower sampling because generation
requires many sequential denoising steps. Recent work on latent diffusion moves
the process into a compressed latent space, dramatically reducing compute while
preserving sample quality, which is the approach behind several widely used
text-to-image systems.

Key takeaways: diffusion models trade sampling speed for training stability and
mode coverage; the core mechanism is learned iterative denoising; and latent-space
variants make the approach practical at scale.
